# Nontraditional Schedules: Firefighter / Shift Work / Military

Do not force a school-calendar-only structure on rotating-schedule parents. Predictability comes from process (monthly calendar exchange, default fallback schedule), not fixed dates.

## Part 1 - Firefighter, Nurse, and Shift-Worker Provisions

Comprehensive Example Provisions for Parenting Plans with
Nontraditional Work Schedules (Firefighters, Nurses, Shift Workers)
Parents with careers involving rotating or extended shifts—like firefighters on Kelly schedules, nurses with
12-hour shifts, or law enforcement officers—face unique scheduling challenges. A parenting plan should
anticipate irregular hours, varying days off, and the need for flexibility. Below are example provisions
tailored for such cases.
1. Acknowledgment of Work Schedule
Nontraditional Hours Clause: The plan acknowledges that the parent works nontraditional shifts
(e.g., 24 on/48 off, Kelly schedule, rotating shifts, or 12-hour shifts).
Schedule Priority: Timesharing will be scheduled in alignment with the parent’s off-duty days
whenever possible.
2. Parenting Time Based on Shift Cycles
Kelly Day Integration: For firefighters, parenting time may align with Kelly days (the designated
rotating day off every third shift cycle).
Rotating Schedules: If a parent’s schedule rotates monthly, the plan shall be reviewed each month
to align timesharing with new shifts.
Consistent Blocks: Parenting time should occur in blocks that maximize quality time (e.g., two or
three consecutive off-duty days).
3. Flexible Scheduling Provisions
Monthly Calendar Exchange: Each parent shall exchange work schedules by the 20th of the
preceding month.
Custom Parenting Calendar: Based on the work schedule, parents shall create a monthly parenting
calendar by the 25th of each month.
Default Schedule: If no agreement is reached, a default alternating weekend plan applies.
4. Holidays & Special Days
Holiday Priority: Holidays may supersede regular shift-based schedules.
Shift Conflicts: If a parent is scheduled to work on their holiday, the holiday may be swapped with
the next available day off.
Kelly Day Adjustment: If a Kelly day falls on a holiday, the parent may exercise extended time
including the adjacent off-duty period.

15. First Right of Refusal
Shift Coverage Clause: If the parenting parent is called into mandatory overtime, the other parent
shall have the first right of refusal to provide care during that time.
Short-Notice Work: Both parents agree to cooperate when unexpected shift extensions or call-ins
occur.
6. Overnight Parenting Time
Post-Shift Rest: If a parent works a 24-hour shift, the following 12 hours may be reserved for
recovery before resuming parenting time, unless mutually agreed otherwise.
Safe Transition Clause: Parents will prioritize the child’s safety and the working parent’s need for
rest when transitioning after long shifts.
7. Communication & Adjustments
Monthly Adjustment Clause: The plan may be modified monthly to align with rotating shifts
without requiring a court order.
Good-Faith Flexibility: Both parents agree to cooperate in good faith to adjust schedules around
changing work demands.
8. Child-Centered Provisions
Consistency in Schooling: Parenting time shall not interfere with the child’s school attendance or
essential extracurricular activities.
Routine Stability: Efforts shall be made to preserve consistent wake-up, bedtime, and activity
routines despite varying parental schedules.
9. Emergency & Mandatory Duty Provisions
Mandatory Overtime: If the parent is held over unexpectedly, the other parent shall be notified
promptly and offered additional parenting time.
Disaster Response Clause: For first responders (firefighters, EMTs, law enforcement), timesharing
may be suspended during declared emergencies, with make-up time provided once the parent’s
duties return to normal.
10. Example Legal Language
“The parenting plan shall accommodate the firefighter parent’s Kelly schedule. Timesharing
shall occur on the parent’s designated Kelly days, consecutive off-duty days, and holidays as
available. Each month, both parents shall exchange schedules and prepare a written calendar.

## Part 2 - Firefighter (Kelly Schedule) Structural Patterns

Parenting Plans for Firefighters:
Managing Time-Sharing When One
Parent Has a Kelly Schedule
When one parent is a firefighter, a traditional custody schedule often does not work.
Most parenting plans are written with a typical Monday-through-Friday work schedule in mind. But
firefighters operate on an entirely different rhythm. Many departments use 24-hour shifts combined
with rotating off-days and scheduled “Kelly days” to balance long hours and prevent burnout.
The result is a schedule that constantly moves through the calendar.
For parents going through a divorce or custody case, this can create a practical question: how do you
design a parenting plan that works when one parent’s work schedule is constantly rotating?
The answer is that courts and families often get creative. Over time, several workable patterns have
emerged that allow firefighters to maintain meaningful time with their children while still meeting the
demands of their profession.
Understanding the Firefighter Schedule
Before discussing parenting plans, it helps to understand how firefighter schedules work.
Many firefighters work a rotation such as 24 hours on duty followed by 48 hours off. Some
departments operate on more complex cycles such as 24 on, 48 off, 24 on, then 96 hours off. To keep
total hours within legal limits, firefighters are also assigned periodic “Kelly days,” which are scheduled
days off that rotate throughout the year.
Because Kelly days shift across the calendar, the firefighter’s available parenting time may not fall on
the same days every week.This is why standard custody schedules often need modification.
Example 1: Parenting Time Based on Off-Duty Days
One of the simplest approaches is to structure parenting time around the firefighter’s off-duty periods.
In this model, the firefighter has the child whenever they are off duty, and the other parent has the
child during the firefighter’s 24-hour shifts.
For example, if the firefighter works Monday morning through Tuesday morning, the child stays with
the other parent during that shift. When the firefighter comes off duty Tuesday morning, the child
transitions back for the firefighter’s off days.
This arrangement closely follows the firefighter’s real schedule and can produce relatively balanced
time between parents.
Example 2: A Traditional Weekly Schedule with Work Overrides
Some families prefer more predictability for school routines. In those situations, the parenting plan may
follow a traditional weekly structure while allowing the firefighter’s work schedule to override specific
days.
For example, one parent might have Monday through Thursday while the other has Thursday through
Monday every week.
If the firefighter is scheduled to work during their parenting time, the other parent temporarily covers
that period and the firefighter receives make-up time tied to their next available off-duty period or
Kelly day.
This allows the child’s weekly routine to remain consistent while still accommodating the firefighter’s
job.
Example 3: Kelly Days as Automatic Parenting Time
Kelly days often become valuable parenting opportunities.
Some parenting plans specifically state that when a Kelly day occurs, the firefighter receives parenting
time during that day even if it falls during the other parent’s usual time.
For example, if the firefighter’s Kelly day falls on a Wednesday, the child may spend that afternoon and
evening with the firefighter.In many families, Kelly days become regular midweek parenting time that would not otherwise exist in
a traditional schedule.
Example 4: Rotating Block Schedules
In some cases, the parenting plan mirrors the firefighter’s full shift rotation.
Rather than trying to force a fixed weekly pattern, the parenting schedule follows the firefighter’s
repeating work cycle. The firefighter has parenting time during extended off-duty blocks, such as 48-
hour or 96-hour periods, depending on the department’s schedule.
Although this schedule may look unusual on paper, it becomes predictable once it is aligned with the
firefighter’s rotation calendar.
Example 5: Alternating Weekends with Flexible Midweek
Overnights
Another approach is to maintain a simple structure while adding flexibility.
Under this type of schedule, the firefighter may have every other weekend along with one or two
midweek overnights when they are off duty.
If the firefighter is working during an assigned overnight, the parenting plan allows that overnight to
shift to the nearest available off-duty day.
Kelly days often become the opportunity for additional midweek parenting time.
What Courts Actually Care About
When courts review parenting plans involving firefighters or other shift workers, the focus is usually
not on whether the schedule looks traditional.
Instead, courts look at three practical factors.
First, the schedule must provide stability for the child. Children benefit from routines, especially
during the school week.
Second, both parents should have meaningful and consistent time with the child whenever
possible.
Third, the schedule must be clear enough that both parents understand exactly when exchanges
occur.In firefighter cases, one of the most effective solutions is to incorporate the department’s shift calendar
directly into the parenting plan so both parents can refer to the same schedule.
Parenting Plans Should Reflect Real Life
Modern families do not always follow traditional work schedules. Firefighters, nurses, airline pilots, and
many other professionals work shifts that rotate through the calendar.
A well-written parenting plan recognizes this reality rather than trying to force a parent into a schedule
that does not match their job.
When parenting plans are built around real work schedules, they tend to reduce conflict and create a
more workable long-term arrangement for both parents and children.
Bottom Line
If one parent is a firefighter with a rotating schedule and Kelly days, there are many ways to structure
parenting time that still prioritize the child’s stability.
The most successful parenting plans usually build around the firefighter’s actual work schedule,
incorporate Kelly days where appropriate, and maintain enough predictability to support school and
daily routines.
Every family’s circumstances are different, which is why parenting plans often need to be customized
rather than relying on a one-size-fits-all approach.

## Part 3 - Military Parenting Plan Provisions

Comprehensive Example Provisions for Military Parenting Plans
Below is a collection of potential provisions that can be adapted for a military parent's parenting plan. These
provisions anticipate deployments, relocations, sudden availability, and other unique challenges of military
service. They should be customized based on the child’s needs, the parents’ circumstances, and
jurisdictional law.
1. Deployment & Temporary Duty (TDY) Provisions
Delegation of Timesharing: During deployment or TDY, the child shall reside primarily with the
non-deployed parent, unless otherwise agreed.
Notice of Deployment: The military parent shall provide written notice of deployment or TDY orders
to the other parent within 7 days of receiving them.
Automatic Reinstatement: Upon return from deployment or TDY, the parenting schedule
automatically reverts to the pre-deployment schedule without further court action.
Virtual Contact: The child shall have reasonable access (video calls, phone, email) with the deployed
parent, as permitted by military service conditions.
2. Relocation & Permanent Change of Station (PCS)
Advance Notice: The military parent shall notify the other parent of PCS orders within 10 days of
receipt.
Relocation Protocol: If PCS requires moving more than 100 miles from the child’s residence, the
plan shall shift to the long-distance parenting schedule.
Transportation Costs: Travel expenses related to long-distance timesharing shall be shared equally,
unless otherwise agreed.
Reversion Clause: If the military parent later relocates back within the designated radius (e.g., 50
miles of the child’s residence), the local schedule shall automatically resume.
3. Sudden Availability Provisions
Short-Notice Parenting Time: If the military parent becomes unexpectedly available (due to early
release, leave, or change in orders), they may request timesharing with at least 48 hours’ notice.
Reasonable Accommodation: The other parent shall make reasonable efforts to accommodate,
provided it does not disrupt school, health, or significant pre-scheduled activities.
Bonus Time: Missed parenting time due to service obligations may be made up within 6 months of
return, provided it is in the child’s best interest.

14. Dual-Track Parenting Plan (Local vs. Long-Distance)
Trigger Clause: If the military parent resides within 50 miles of the child’s primary residence, the
local plan shall apply. If reassigned beyond that range, the long-distance plan shall apply.
Local Plan Example: Alternating weekends, one midweek overnight, and shared holidays.
Long-Distance Plan Example: Extended summer timesharing, alternating holidays, and monthly
visits if feasible.
5. Virtual & Electronic Contact
Guaranteed Access: Both parents shall facilitate reasonable virtual contact (video calls, phone,
messaging) at least 3 times per week when distance prevents in-person time.
Time Zone Flexibility: Virtual contact shall consider military time zones and availability.
Recording Alternative: If real-time contact is not possible, the military parent may send recorded
messages for the child to view.
6. Holidays & Leave Periods
Block Leave: If the military parent receives block leave, they may exercise extended parenting time
during that period.
Make-Up Holidays: Missed holidays due to deployment may be rescheduled within 90 days of
return.
Priority During R&R: During military Rest & Recuperation (R&R) leave, the military parent shall have
priority parenting time.
7. First Right of Refusal
If the non-military parent requires childcare exceeding 6 hours, the military parent shall be offered
the opportunity to care for the child before third parties.
8. Decision-Making Authority During Absence
Delegation of Authority: During deployment, the military parent may delegate day-to-day decision-making to a designated relative or spouse, while ultimate legal decision-making remains with the
parents.
Medical & School Decisions: The non-deployed parent shall have temporary authority to make
necessary medical and educational decisions during deployment.

29. Stability & Non-Penalization
Non-Penalization Clause: Military service obligations, including deployment and relocation, shall
not be used as the sole basis to permanently alter parental rights.
SCRA Protection: The plan shall comply with the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act (SCRA) to protect
the military parent’s rights during active duty.
10. Dispute Resolution & Flexibility
Expedited Mediation: Any disputes about schedule changes due to military service shall first be
submitted to expedited mediation before litigation.
Rapid-Change Clause: Both parties acknowledge that military orders may change on short notice
and agree to cooperate in good faith to adjust the parenting schedule accordingly.
Closing Note
These provisions can be mixed, matched, or customized. The guiding principles should always be: 1.
Maximize the child’s relationship with both parents. 2. Respect the unpredictability of military life. 3. Provide
clarity to avoid constant court intervention.
